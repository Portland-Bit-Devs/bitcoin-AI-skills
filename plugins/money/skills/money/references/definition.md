# What Money Is

## The problem money solves

Trade lets people specialize, and specialization raises output — which is why we call
occupations "trades." But direct exchange (barter) requires a **coincidence of interests**:
each party must hold precisely what the other wants, in the right amount, in the right
place, at the right time. In small groups that's workable. As groups grow, the requirement
becomes the binding constraint on how complex an economy can get.

Money is the solution: it converts direct exchange into **indirect exchange**. You no
longer need to find someone who wants your apples and has the shoes you want. You sell
apples for money and buy shoes with money.

So: **the purpose of money is to facilitate trade, which lets groups specialize and
organize with greater complexity.**

Barter also scales badly for a second reason — pricing. With `n` goods and direct
exchange, there are `n²` exchange rates to track. With a common monetary unit, there are
`n` prices.

## Salability

Carl Menger's term (*On the Origins of Money*, 1892): a good's **salability** is its
ability to be sold in a given market at the time and price the seller desires.

Market participants converge on the most salable good over many iterations of trade — not
by decree, but because each individually prefers to hold whatever is most likely to be
accepted later.

The classic illustration: an apple merchant sells what apples he can, then trades the
remainder at a discount for something less perishable, knowing his apples will rot. The
merchant who takes them does the same thing one step further along, trading into something
still more widely accepted. Repeated across a market, this ratchets everyone toward a
single good — the one that best holds value and is most widely accepted. That good becomes
money. People then acquire it not for its own usefulness but for the **certainty** that it
can be exchanged for what they want later.

## Declining marginal utility

Menger offered a second framing that sharpens the first. The most salable good is the one
with the **lowest rate of declining marginal utility** — the good whose tenth unit is worth
nearly as much to you as its first.

- **High** decline: a house. The second one is worth far less to you than the first.
- **Medium** decline: electricity. Roughly constant value until everything is powered.
- **Low** decline: whatever becomes money. Each additional unit is worth about what the
  last one was.

Put memorably: money is the good you can't get enough of.

## The three dimensions of salability

Saifedean Ammous (*The Bitcoin Standard*) decomposes salability into three dimensions,
each dissolving one of barter's coincidences:

| Dimension | What it means | Coincidence removed |
|---|---|---|
| **Across time** | Holds value into the future | Timing — pots beat apples |
| **Across space** | Moves easily over distance | Location — digital beats physical |
| **Across scales** | Groups and divides cleanly | Amount — water beats a house |

## The three functions

A good acquires the three monetary functions **in order**, and each maps to one dimension:

1. **Store of value** — maintains value over time. *(Salability across time.)*
2. **Medium of exchange** — acquired to trade again rather than to consume.
   *(Salability across space.)*
3. **Unit of account** — the common measure prices are quoted in. *(Across scales.)*

The sequence is causal, not incidental. First a good must be worth holding — people must
reasonably expect its demand won't deteriorate. As more people notice this, they begin
trading it among themselves. Wide acceptance becomes self-fulfilling: it is accepted
*because* it is accepted. Only once it circulates broadly do merchants start quoting prices
in it, and it becomes the unit of account.

This is why a candidate money that is volatile against the incumbent unit of account is not
thereby disqualified — it may simply be earlier in the sequence.

## Monetary value vs. market value

A crucial distinction, and the source of a lot of confused argument.

- **Market value** derives from a good's **consumption** utility — what it's worth to
  someone who will use it.
- **Monetary value** derives from a good's **monetary properties** — what it's worth to
  someone who will trade it onward.

The worked example: you sell a car. Jim wants to drive it and offers $20,000. Joe is a
dealer who plans to resell it, and offers $15,000. The car's consumption value is $20,000;
its trade value is $15,000. That $5,000 gap is the implicit economic cost of using the car
as a medium of exchange.

**Money is the good for which those two values are equal.** Nobody demands a discount to
take money off your hands, because it is already the most tradeable thing available. The
better a society's money, the smaller these frictions across the whole economy.

Note the dependency runs one way: market value exists without money, but a monetary medium
can't exist without underlying goods and services that have market value. Money gets its
value by *enabling the exchange* of things that have consumption value.

## Convergence and network effects

Convergence accelerates as it proceeds. The more participants use a given medium, the more
likely any new participant is to use it too — the same dynamic that concentrates social
networks despite near-identical functionality across platforms.

In a frictionless world this drives toward a single money. In the real world, several
persist, because of:

- **Information opacity** — societies historically unaware of each other's currencies.
  Largely dissolved by the internet.
- **Sovereign coercion** — national currencies used because their use is enforced. Still
  very much present, and the reason most people never consciously choose a money at all.
- **Genuine tradeoffs** — different monetary goods really do excel on different properties.

That last one is worth taking seriously rather than treating as pure friction: competition
between monies is what protects users from a monetary monopoly.

## Fiat

*Fiat* means "by decree." Fiat money is money by mandate rather than by merit — chosen for
users rather than converged upon by them. That's a descriptive claim about mechanism, not
by itself a verdict on quality: evaluate any fiat currency on the same six properties as
anything else.
